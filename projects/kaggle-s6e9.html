Kaggle Playground S6E9

A 3-day sprint on a synthetic tabular competition predicting EV purchase intent — ending top-10% on the public leaderboard by prioritizing feature engineering and careful blending over exotic models.

PythonLightGBMCatBoostEnsembling

The competition

Kaggle's Playground series runs short competitions on synthetic tabular data, scored by ROC-AUC. This one: predict whether a person will buy an electric vehicle from a set of demographic and behavioral features. I had about three days before the deadline, which meant the workflow had to prioritize what actually moves the score, not everything that theoretically could.

Don't skip the literature review

Before writing a line of modeling code, I searched for public write-ups and repos tackling the same competition. One turned up a full, honest pipeline that reproduced to six decimal places when I ran it myself — confirmation it wasn't overfit to the public leaderboard by luck. Starting from a verified-honest baseline instead of re-deriving the same ideas from scratch was, by a wide margin, the single biggest time-saver of the whole sprint.

What actually moved the score

In order of actual impact, not of how interesting each idea sounded going in:

  • Synthetic-data artifact features — this dataset was generated, not collected, and generated tabular data tends to leak patterns in how values were produced (digit patterns, value counts, nested target encoding). These gave the single biggest score jump of anything I tried.
  • 20-fold shallow gradient boosting — LightGBM and CatBoost, bagged across many folds and seeds rather than tuned for a single best model.
  • Blending the two model families by rank, not raw probability — gradient boosting families tend to be correlated in their mistakes, and a rank-based blend handles that better than a naive average.
  • Hill-climbing a blend against public out-of-fold predictions from other competitors' shared datasets, nested-validated so the blend weights weren't just overfitting to those specific public submissions.

One idea that looked promising but added nothing: pulling in an external, non-synthetic EV-adoption dataset and computing per-value adoption rates from it as extra features. Zero measurable gain — a reminder that "more data" only helps when it's actually informative for this dataset's generation process, not just topically related.

Picking the two final submissions

Kaggle lets you select two submissions to be scored for final placement, and the safe strategy is to hedge rather than submit your single best-looking score twice. I went with one submission that was my own honest, fully cross-validated blend, and a second that leaned more on a public-leaderboard-tuned anchor mix as a hedge against the private leaderboard shuffling rankings. That combination landed at roughly the top 8% of entrants.

Discipline that mattered more than any single feature: only submitting when a new version's validation score beat the last submitted one by a meaningful margin, not just noise — and always sanity-checking the submission file's shape and row IDs before uploading, since a silently misaligned submission scores badly for reasons that have nothing to do with the model.
